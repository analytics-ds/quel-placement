Choosing between the PEA and life insurance means comparing two wrappers built for different needs. The Plan d'Epargne en Actions targets stock market performance with taxation that rewards holding time, while life insurance combines security, flexibility and inheritance planning. This comparison breaks down the criteria that matter most for choosing based on your profile.

## PEA and life insurance: two wrappers with different objectives

The PEA is an account dedicated to equity investing, designed to encourage long-term holding of European securities. It can only be opened once per adult who is a French tax resident, and closes automatically on early withdrawal from the standard version. Each taxpayer can hold only one standard PEA, optionally complemented by a PEA-PME.

Life insurance is a more versatile savings contract. It allows part of the capital to be secured in a guaranteed fund while also giving access to financial markets through unit-linked funds, with no limit on the number of contracts held.

The PEA therefore targets capital growth on equity markets, while life insurance combines several objectives at once: partial precautionary savings, diversification and inheritance planning.

## Which investment universe for the PEA and life insurance

One of the most decisive criteria for telling the two wrappers apart is the diversity of accessible investment vehicles.

### What the PEA can hold

The standard PEA is restricted to shares of European companies, eligible ETFs and certain funds invested mostly in equities. The PEA-PME extends access to small and mid-cap securities, with a separate contribution cap.

### What life insurance can hold

Life insurance gives access to a broader universe: capital-guaranteed funds, unit-linked funds invested in equities, bonds, real estate funds (SCPI) or collective investment funds (OPCVM). This diversity makes it possible to adjust the contract's risk level according to the investment horizon and tolerance for capital loss. A multi-fund contract also allows contributions to be spread across several funds, and that allocation can be rebalanced during the life of the contract without closing it or triggering immediate taxation.

<div class="callout-asavoir">
The same vehicle, such as a European equity ETF, can sometimes be held both in a PEA and as a unit-linked fund within life insurance. The choice of wrapper then mostly depends on taxation and the intended holding period.
</div>

## Taxation of the PEA and life insurance: what changes before and after the threshold

Taxation is the criterion that carries the most weight in this decision, as it rewards patience in both cases, but under different rules.

### PEA taxation before and after 5 years

Withdrawing before 5 years generally closes the plan and triggers taxation of the gains at the flat 30 percent tax rate, except in cases of force majeure defined by law (dismissal, disability, death). After 5 years, gains are exempt from income tax: only the 17.2 percent social contributions remain due at withdrawal.

<div class="callout-attention">
Withdrawing before the 5-year threshold remains the main mistake to avoid with a PEA: it cancels the intended tax advantage and closes the plan, with no possibility of opening a second one personally.
</div>

### Life insurance taxation before and after 8 years

Before 8 years, gains withdrawn are subject to the flat 30 percent tax rate (or, on request, to the progressive income tax scale). After 8 years, an annual allowance applies to withdrawn gains before taxation, and the tax rate drops for the remaining portion. In both cases, only the share of gains included in the withdrawal is taxed, not the capital originally paid in.

## Can a PEA and life insurance be combined

Nothing prevents holding both wrappers in parallel, and it is in fact the most common setup among savers who diversify their strategy. The PEA then acts as the dedicated vehicle for stock market performance, while life insurance handles the secured portion and estate planning.

Combining both also spreads the tax exposure: the PEA's contribution caps (150,000 euros for the standard PEA) are independent from life insurance, which has no cap at all. In practice, a saver might prioritise the PEA while targeting stock market growth over 10 or 15 years, while keeping a life insurance contract open for several years to benefit as early as possible from the 8-year threshold and prepare a future transfer.

## Inheritance: what happens to a PEA or life insurance policy on death

When the holder dies, the PEA is automatically closed. The securities it holds are transferred to a standard securities account and enter the estate under ordinary inheritance rules, with no preferential tax treatment.

Life insurance, by contrast, benefits from a specific transfer framework: capital paid to named beneficiaries largely escapes standard inheritance tax, within an allowance per beneficiary for amounts paid in before age 70. This is one of life insurance's main advantages over the PEA when inheritance planning is a priority.

## PEA vs life insurance: comparison table and typical profiles

| Criterion | PEA | Life insurance |
|---|---|---|
| Investment universe | European equities, ETFs and eligible funds | Guaranteed funds, unit-linked funds, SCPI, OPCVM |
| Favourable taxation | After 5 years (income tax exemption) | After 8 years (annual allowance, then reduced rate) |
| Early withdrawal | Generally closes the plan | Partial withdrawal possible without closing |
| Contribution cap | 150,000 euros (standard PEA) | No cap |
| Inheritance | Ordinary inheritance rules | Allowance per beneficiary, outside standard estate rules |

The PEA suits a profile aiming for long-term stock market growth who accepts locking up funds for 5 years. Life insurance suits a profile seeking flexibility, broader diversification and inheritance planning.

The PEA and life insurance are not competitors but complements: the first optimises stock market performance with taxation that rewards the first 5 years, the second combines versatility and inheritance planning with an 8-year tax threshold.
</div>
